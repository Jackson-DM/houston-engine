Most people assume the best AI practitioners came up through software engineering. The ones actually shipping production-grade systems came from somewhere else entirely.

I have spent the last several years working with executive teams on AI adoption — strategy sessions, implementation roadmaps, organizational change, and the hard conversations about what gets automated and what does not. My background is not in computer science. It is in building the conditions under which complex decisions get made well. That background turned out to be the exact right foundation for what enterprise AI actually demands.

THE STRATEGIC LENS ADVANTAGE

AI implementation at scale is not a technical problem. It is a coordination problem wrapped in a technical costume.

The decisions that determine whether an AI deployment succeeds or fails are fundamentally about clarity: Who owns the outcome? What failure looks like. How you define success before you start. These are questions that strategic and consulting disciplines have been refining for decades.

When I work with a leadership team deploying AI into their workflows, the most common failure mode is not a bad model or a flawed integration. It is ambiguity that never got resolved. The team that spent six months building a document processing agent often failed not because the technology did not work, but because nobody agreed on what "accurate enough" meant, who had sign-off authority when the system surfaced a conflict, and what the escalation path looked like when the AI was wrong.

Executives and consultants who have managed large-scale transformation programs recognize this pattern immediately. We have seen it in ERP rollouts, acquisition integrations, and regulatory overhauls. The shape of the failure is the same. The discipline that prevents it is the same.

THE OPERATING ASSUMPTION NOBODY TALKS ABOUT

There is an unspoken assumption in most AI conversations: that technical fluency is the primary credential. If you can explain how attention mechanisms work, you belong in the room. If you cannot, defer to someone who can.

This assumption is costing organizations real money.

The people who understand how a transformer model works mathematically are not always the people who can tell you whether a given deployment will survive contact with the actual business. That requires a different set of skills — stakeholder mapping, incentive analysis, process documentation, change management, and the ability to read an organization's tolerance for ambiguity.

I have sat in rooms where extraordinarily capable engineers presented technically sound AI solutions that had no realistic path to adoption. Not because the technology was wrong. Because the proposal did not account for the organizational dynamics that would determine whether anyone would actually use it.

Strategic and executive experience is not a consolation prize for people who cannot code. It is a primary qualification for the work that determines whether AI investments pay off.

BUILDING IN PUBLIC AS AN EXECUTIVE DISCIPLINE

There is a version of building in public that looks like posting process screenshots and model comparisons. That version is useful for practitioners early in their journey.

There is a more powerful version: documenting the strategic decisions, the organizational experiments, and the failure modes that do not show up in vendor case studies.

When I share observations about why a particular AI governance structure failed, or what actually happened when a leadership team tried to delegate a core decision to an automated system, I am not just creating content. I am contributing to the field in a way that pure technical documentation cannot. The human and organizational dimensions of AI deployment are underreported because they are harder to make into a benchmark.

Executives who build in public — who share what they are learning about AI adoption at the leadership level — fill a genuine gap. There are not enough honest accounts of what it looks like to integrate AI into a real organization with real politics, real constraints, and real accountability structures.

THE INFRASTRUCTURE CONVERSATION EXECUTIVES OWN

The industry has crossed a threshold. AI is no longer a research topic or a pilot program category. It is infrastructure. The Stargate initiative, the scale of enterprise compute investment, the shift to agent-native architectures — these are signals that the question has moved from "should we adopt AI" to "how do we build the operating model for AI at scale."

That is an executive conversation. It belongs in the same strategic planning processes as supply chain resilience, talent architecture, and capital allocation. The organizations that treat AI infrastructure decisions as IT procurement decisions will fall behind the ones that treat them as enterprise strategy.

The competitive advantage in this environment does not go to the company with the best model. It goes to the company with the clearest operating model for how AI decisions get made, governed, and iterated.

HOW TO TRANSLATE YOUR OWN STORY

If you are coming from a background in management consulting, operations, finance, legal, or any other domain that has not been traditionally associated with technology, the translation is simpler than you think.

You already understand how organizations behave under pressure. You already know how to structure a decision that has to survive scrutiny from multiple stakeholders. You already have the vocabulary for risk, governance, and accountability.

Those are not soft skills peripheral to AI work. They are the hard skills that determine whether AI work produces durable business value or impressive demos that never make it past the pilot stage.

Stop positioning your background as "despite not being technical." Start positioning it as the discipline that makes the technology legible to the organization and the organization ready for the technology.

The field needs more practitioners who understand AI at the intersection of strategy and implementation — not one or the other. The executives and consultants who move now, who invest in understanding what these systems can and cannot do, and who bring their organizational fluency to bear on the deployment problem, will define what leadership in the AI era actually looks like.
