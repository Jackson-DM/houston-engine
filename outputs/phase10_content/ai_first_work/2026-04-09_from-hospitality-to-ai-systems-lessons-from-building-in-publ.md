Most people think my transition from hospitality management to building AI systems was a pivot. In reality, it was just a change in medium.

I spent years on the floor of high-end restaurants. It is an environment where seconds matter, mistakes are visible, and the system is only as strong as its most recent interaction. If you lose your cool when a guest receives the wrong order or a kitchen station goes down during a rush, you lose the night.

When I started building AI agents and workflows, I expected to be overwhelmed by technical jargon and complex architecture. Instead, I found myself right at home. The skills that once kept a dining room functioning are the exact same ones required to deploy robust AI infrastructure today.

THE HOSPITALITY BLUEPRINT

Service design is systems design. In hospitality, you map the guest journey—from the moment they walk through the door to the final bill. You identify friction points, automate the routine steps to save time, and leave the human touch for the moments that require genuine empathy.

AI work is a digital reflection of this. When I map out a process for an AI agent, I am essentially doing table management. I am looking for bottlenecks, anticipating failure states, and ensuring the delivery of information is frictionless. If your AI isn't delivering immediate, tangible value, it is no different than a waiter who forgets to refill a water glass. The system failed because the design was not intuitive.

Operational discipline—the kind you learn when you have three hundred covers and a missing line cook—is the most underrated trait in the enterprise AI space. We are seeing companies like ENEOS Materials hit eighty percent productivity gains not just because they have the right tools, but because they have the right deployment instincts. They treat AI implementation like a live service, moving with precision, testing in production, and iterating based on real-world feedback.

THE POWER OF BUILDING IN PUBLIC

The leap into AI can feel lonely. The landscape is moving so fast that imposter syndrome is practically a job requirement. My solution was to start building in public.

I realized early on that nobody cares about your resume; they care about what you can ship. By documenting my process—the failures, the API bottlenecks, the prompt tuning, and the deployment headaches—I stopped being a salesperson for my skills and started being a provider of evidence.

Building in public creates a portfolio of proof. It turns your career transition into an open-source development project. When you share how you solved a specific workflow problem using a model, you arent just showing off; you are providing a roadmap for others. This transparency does two things: it builds immediate credibility with potential collaborators, and it accelerates your own learning by inviting critique from people who have been in the trenches longer than you.

THE INFRASTRUCTURE REALITY

If you pay attention to the current pulse of the industry, you see that we are shifting from the hype cycle to the infrastructure phase. From projects like the Stargate compute initiative to the massive efforts to onshore hardware production with partners like Foxconn, the message is clear: AI is no longer a software experiment. It is a utility.

As these systems become as fundamental as electricity, the people who thrive will be the ones who understand how to connect the energy to the application. This is where the old-school hospitality mindset becomes a superpower. When the grid capacity requires deliberate, large-scale investment, and enterprises are pivoting to agent-native architectures, you stop looking at AI as a magic trick and start looking at it as a supply chain.

HOW TO TRANSLATE YOUR OWN STORY

If you are currently trying to bridge the gap between a non-technical background and an AI career, stop trying to obscure your past. Do not hide your experience in retail, logistics, teaching, or management.

Frame your past as your competitive advantage.

If you worked in customer support, you understand edge cases better than any developer who has never spoken to a client. Use that. If you worked in supply chain management, you understand the necessity of uptime and robust pipelines. Use that. The AI industry does not need more people who can explain how a transformer model works mathematically. We have plenty of those. We need more people who understand the operational reality of how to integrate these tools into existing human systems without breaking the business.

When I talk to hiring managers now, I do not talk about my interest in AI. I talk about my ability to design service layers that function under pressure. I talk about how I learned to troubleshoot under the heat of a Saturday night dinner service, and how that translates to identifying why a prompt chain failed in production.

Honesty is a force multiplier. When you acknowledge that you are learning, building, and iterating in full view of the public, you attract the right kind of mentors and the right kind of opportunities. You stop chasing jobs and start shaping the systems you want to work within.

We are currently in a period where experience is being re-evaluated through the lens of automation. The most valuable professionals of the next decade wont be the ones who know every nuance of the latest model release. They will be the ones who can take a broken, high-pressure process and turn it into a high-functioning system.

Whether it is a dining room floor or a server rack, the fundamentals of excellence remain identical: anticipate the needs of the user, clear the bottlenecks, and deliver the service with absolute reliability.
